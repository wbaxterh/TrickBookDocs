## Nobody planned all of this

Skateboarding did not begin with a rulebook, a league, or one person announcing that they had invented a new sport. Its early history is messier than that.

People experimented with wheeled boards in different places. Surfers in California are strongly connected to the story because they wanted something to ride when the waves were flat. Roller-skate wheels were attached to pieces of wood, and “sidewalk surfing” gave people a way to carve on land.

The first versions were basic. Some used hard steel or composite wheels. The ride was noisy, rough, and not especially forgiving. A pebble could end a session faster than a concerned parent.

> **Mack's Call**
>
> Early skateboarders deserve credit for courage. They looked at a plank with metal wheels and thought, “This should probably go faster.”

There is no perfectly neat answer to “Who invented skateboarding?” That is worth remembering. Skateboarding often develops through many people trying related ideas, borrowing what works, and changing it for their own terrain.

## The first wave

By the early 1960s, manufacturers were selling commercial skateboards, surf companies were building teams, and organized contests were beginning to appear. Early competitive skating included freestyle and slalom. Freestyle emphasized balance, footwork, spins, handstands, and inventive movement on flat ground. Slalom challenged riders to weave through cones at speed.

Patti McGee became one of the first widely recognized stars of this period. She won the women's division at the 1964 National Skateboard Championships and became the first female professional skateboarder in 1965. Her handstand appeared on the cover of *LIFE*, giving millions of people an image of skateboarding that was athletic, unusual, and difficult to ignore.

Skateboarding's first surge did not last. Equipment had limits, injuries drew concern, and the public moved on to other interests. This would become a familiar pattern: skateboarding grows, people declare it a craze, the craze supposedly ends, and a smaller group keeps skating anyway.

> **Kaori's Notes**
>
> A drop in popularity is not the same as disappearance. Skateboarding repeatedly survives in garages, driveways, schoolyards, local scenes, and the work of small companies.

## A better wheel changes the terrain

In the early 1970s, Frank Nasworthy adapted polyurethane roller-skate wheels for skateboards and sold them under the Cadillac Wheels name. Compared with the hard wheels that came before them, urethane wheels were smoother, quieter, faster, and grippier.

That sounds like a simple equipment upgrade. It changed what a skateboard could do.

Riders gained more control. Rougher pavement became usable. Turns could be pushed harder. Banks, ditches, and transitions became more interesting because the wheels could hold a line instead of sliding without warning.

The lesson shows up throughout skateboarding history: change the equipment and skaters discover new terrain; discover new terrain and they invent new movement.

### Drought, pools, and vertical walls

In California, skaters began riding banks and empty swimming pools. The rounded walls of pools allowed them to carve higher and eventually reach the lip. A space designed for swimming became a three-dimensional riding surface.

The Zephyr Competition Team—often called the Z-Boys—became closely associated with the aggressive, low style emerging from the Dogtown area of Santa Monica and Venice. Their story is important, but it is not the entire story of pool skating or the 1970s. Scenes were developing in different regions, and women including Peggy Oki, Laura Thornhill, Ellen O'Neal, and others were contributing during an era too often reduced to a short list of men.

Purpose-built skateparks followed. Bowls, snake runs, banks, and vertical transitions gave skaters terrain designed for speed and experimentation. Skateboarding was no longer limited to reproducing surfing turns on flat pavement. It was forming its own vocabulary.

## Leaving the ground

One trick changed nearly everything that followed.

In the late 1970s, Alan “Ollie” Gelfand developed a no-handed aerial while riding transition. Instead of grabbing the board, he could rise with it beneath his feet. The trick became known as the ollie.

Rodney Mullen later adapted the ollie to flat ground. That shift helped turn curbs, benches, stairs, gaps, and eventually handrails into possibilities. Mullen also developed or advanced many tricks that became central to modern street skating, including the kickflip, heelflip, and 360 flip.

The ollie did not create street skating by itself, and skateboarding history should be careful about turning collaborative progression into one-hero mythology. But the ability to lift the board from flat ground gave generations of skaters a new tool. A curb was no longer only something to roll off. It could be jumped onto, over, or along.

> **Mack's Call**
>
> Once skaters learned to ollie, city planners accidentally began designing skate obstacles everywhere.

## Ramps, backyard engineering, and skate videos

The late 1970s and 1980s brought another cycle of growth, decline, and reinvention. When some commercial skateparks closed, skaters built ramps themselves. Backyard halfpipes and launch ramps turned construction into part of skate culture.

Vertical skating grew more technical and more visible. Riders developed aerials, inverts, lip tricks, and combinations that would have been difficult to imagine on the earliest boards. At the same time, freestyle continued to generate techniques that street skaters could adapt.

Skate videos became one of the culture's most important forms. A contest showed who performed best under a particular format on a particular day. A video part could show how a skater saw the world.

Videos carried tricks, style, music, clothing, filming methods, and spot selection from one scene to another. A skater in a town with no major contest could watch a tape repeatedly, study a trick frame by frame, and then try a local version of it.

The Bones Brigade videos are among the best-known examples of this era. They combined elite skating, character, music, and storytelling in a form young skaters could replay until the tape wore thin.

> **Kaori's Notes**
>
> Before online clips, skateboarding knowledge traveled through magazines, photographs, VHS tapes, shop videos, contests, demos, and direct observation. A trick could arrive in a new town because one person returned from a trip having seen it.

## The street becomes the park

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, street skating was becoming the dominant form for many skaters. It did not require a giant ramp or a formal facility. Curbs, schoolyards, loading docks, plazas, stairs, and handrails supplied the terrain.

Street skating also changed board design. Shapes became more symmetrical, noses grew more functional, and setups evolved around technical tricks. The distinction between freestyle ideas and street terrain became increasingly porous as skaters combined flatground tricks with obstacles.

This period could look smaller from the outside. Skateboarding's mainstream popularity dipped again, companies folded, and professional opportunities narrowed. Inside the culture, however, progression accelerated. Skater-owned companies, independent magazines, shops, and videos helped decide what mattered.

Style mattered alongside difficulty. The same trick could look completely different depending on speed, height, posture, catch, landing, and the line surrounding it. Skateboarding was not only asking, “Was the trick completed?” It was also asking, “How did it look, and why did this skater choose it here?”

## The world sees skateboarding again

The first Extreme Games—later renamed the X Games—were held in 1995. Television placed skateboarding beside other action sports and gave it a larger international audience. In 1999, Tony Hawk landed a 900 at the X Games after years of attempts, creating one of skateboarding's most widely recognized moments.

That same year, *Tony Hawk's Pro Skater* brought skateboarding into homes through a video game. Players learned trick names, heard new music, discovered skaters, and began looking differently at handrails and stair sets—even if their first real board did not award points for crashing into a wall.

Television and games helped skateboarding grow, but the culture was never created by exposure alone. Local shops, crews, photographers, filmers, magazines, artists, park builders, contest organizers, and skater-owned companies continued to do the daily work.

> **Mack's Call**
>
> A video game can make you want to skate. It cannot make the pavement softer. That part of the experience remains surprisingly realistic.

## Bigger than one country or one kind of skater

Skateboarding's popular history is often told through Southern California. That is an important center of the story, not the border of it.

Skaters around the world developed scenes around the terrain available to them. Brazil, Japan, Europe, Australia, Canada, and many other regions produced distinct styles, companies, contests, publications, and influential riders. Videos and travel connected scenes, but local architecture and culture kept them from becoming identical.

Women and girls had been present throughout skateboarding history, even when coverage, sponsorship, and contest opportunities failed to reflect it. Over time, skaters, organizers, filmmakers, and companies created stronger platforms and made that history harder to overlook.

Adaptive skateboarders also expanded assumptions about how a skateboard and terrain could be used. Their progression is not a side note to “regular” skateboarding. It is part of the same ongoing practice of looking at a board, a body, and an obstacle and finding a new solution.

## From banned activity to Olympic event

In 2016, skateboarding was approved for inclusion in the Tokyo 2020 Olympic Games. Because of the pandemic, its Olympic debut occurred in 2021. The program included street and park events for women and men.

The moment was strange and significant. An activity long associated with “No Skateboarding” signs was now being awarded Olympic medals. Some skaters welcomed the visibility and opportunities. Others worried that formal judging and governing bodies could flatten a culture built around independence.

Both reactions make sense. Skateboarding can be a sport, an art form, transportation, a social world, a profession, or something a person does alone after school. The Olympics did not settle what skateboarding is. They demonstrated how many meanings it had already accumulated.

Skateboarding returned at Paris 2024, where the temporary venue at Place de la Concorde placed street and park competition in the center of the city. By then, the sight of young skaters from different countries sharing an Olympic course felt less like a novelty and more like the visible edge of a much larger global culture.

## History is still being made

Skateboarding does not progress in a straight line. Its popularity rises and falls. Terrain changes. Companies appear and disappear. Tricks once considered impossible become expected, then are reworked by someone with a different style or a new obstacle.

The important part is not memorizing every date before you are allowed to skate. History helps you notice that the board under your feet contains generations of experiments: better wheels, stronger trucks, changing shapes, new tricks, new ways of filming, and new ideas about who belongs.

You are allowed to enter that history as a beginner.

Push down the sidewalk. Learn to turn. Ask what a trick is called. Watch how other people use a park. Eventually, you will develop preferences of your own. That is how skateboarding keeps moving—one person sees what already exists, then tries it a little differently.

> **Mack's Call**
>
> You do not need to know the whole family tree before your first push. Just remember that the board came from somewhere, and leave a little room for the people who arrive after you.

## Timeline for the illustrated spread

- **1940s–1950s:** Early commercial and homemade wheeled-board experiments
- **Early 1960s:** Commercial boards, surf-company teams, freestyle, slalom, and national contests
- **1965:** Patti McGee becomes the first female professional skateboarder
- **Early 1970s:** Polyurethane wheels transform control and terrain
- **Mid-to-late 1970s:** Pool riding and purpose-built skateparks expand transition skating
- **Late 1970s:** Alan Gelfand develops the transition ollie
- **1982:** Rodney Mullen demonstrates the flatground ollie in competition
- **1980s:** Vert, freestyle, backyard ramps, magazines, and skate videos drive progression
- **Late 1980s–1990s:** Modern street skating and skater-owned media reshape the culture
- **1995:** The first Extreme Games bring televised attention
- **1999:** Tony Hawk lands the 900; *Tony Hawk's Pro Skater* is released
- **2016:** Skateboarding is approved for the Tokyo Olympic program
- **2021:** Olympic debut at the delayed Tokyo 2020 Games
- **2024:** Skateboarding returns at the Paris Games
