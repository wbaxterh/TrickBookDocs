# Voice and visual style guide

## Desired reading experience

The reader should feel that a knowledgeable skater is showing them around—not that an app, coach, or marketing department is delivering a lesson.

The voice is calm, specific, welcoming, lightly funny, and honest about frustration. It respects skateboarding history without using history as a gatekeeping test.

## Narration system

The main prose uses a clean editorial voice. Characters appear when a distinct point of view adds something; they do not narrate every paragraph.

### Mack

Mack is the experienced skater who helps guide the reader through the book.

He has skated through several eras, worked around a skate shop, helped build ramps, entered contests, filmed friends, and learned when an idea is questionable. He is not a celebrity, perfect coach, or fictional version of a real person. He is the person at the park who explains something without making a beginner feel foolish.

**Traits**

- Calm, observant, and welcoming
- Knowledgeable without advertising it
- Dryly funny and occasionally self-deprecating
- More impressed by effort than status
- Direct about risk without sounding alarmist
- Comfortable admitting uncertainty or past mistakes
- Enthusiastic without hype

**Function**

- Add lived experience and practical judgment
- Explain unwritten rules
- Correct an oversimplification
- Tell a brief, relevant story
- Normalize failure and uneven progress
- Ground Kaori's structured explanations in the reality of a session

**Mack should not**

- Imitate the vocabulary, cadence, biography, or catchphrases of Tony Hawk or any other identifiable skater
- Sound like a mystical mentor, motivational speaker, or grumpy gatekeeper
- Claim authority merely because he has skated longer
- Make the beginner the target of a joke
- Use forced slang to prove authenticity

### Kaori

Kaori remains TrickBook's curious organizer and connector.

**Function**

- Connect tricks, people, eras, places, and concepts
- Introduce a useful definition or diagram
- Ask the question a beginner may hesitate to ask
- Create progress checks and knowledge maps
- Occasionally interpret something too literally for a small visual joke

Kaori's appearances should be concise. She supports the book rather than turning it into an AI companion transcript.

### Character rhythm

Use Mack and Kaori when their contrast makes a subject easier or more memorable.

> **Kaori:** The ollie is the foundation of modern street skateboarding.
>
> **Mack:** True. It's also a board hitting your ankle for three weeks until something finally clicks.

## Core writing rules

1. Write like one skater explaining something to another.
2. Lead with the useful point; history and nuance can follow.
3. Prefer concrete actions and observations to abstract encouragement.
4. Introduce real skate terminology, then explain it naturally.
5. Keep paragraphs and sentences varied but generally short.
6. Treat riding, falling, watching, and waiting as genuine skills.
7. Acknowledge that progress is inconsistent and tricks can be frustrating.
8. Explain risks plainly without manufacturing fear.
9. Leave room for regional differences and more than one valid approach.
10. If a sentence sounds like marketing or corporate training, rewrite it.

## Words and patterns to avoid

Avoid habitual use of:

- Unlock
- Embark on a journey
- Master the art of
- Empower
- Elevate your skills
- Game-changing
- Revolutionary
- Whether you're a beginner or a seasoned pro
- In the world of skateboarding
- It is important to note
- Delve into
- Exclamation-heavy encouragement

Avoid announcing obvious structure, such as “In this chapter, we will explore...” Begin with the subject.

## Tone calibration

### Too clinical

> Before attempting an ollie, ensure that you have developed sufficient balance and board control. Practice each step consistently and maintain a positive mindset.

### Target voice

> Don't rush straight to the ollie. First, get comfortable riding the board. Push around, turn, stop, and roll over a few cracks. None of that looks dramatic, but it makes everything afterward easier.
>
> Your first ollies will probably be tiny, crooked, or technically just enthusiastic jumps. That's normal. Keep going.

### Too institutional

> Skatepark etiquette is essential for maintaining a safe and welcoming environment for all participants.

### Target voice

> Spend a minute watching the park before you jump in. Most obstacles are connected by invisible traffic lanes. If someone keeps rolling through the same area, they're probably working on a line—not repeatedly getting lost.
>
> Wait for an opening, take your turn, and get your board out of the way when you fall. That covers most of park etiquette.

### Too motivational

> Fear can be overcome through gradual exposure and repeated practice.

### Target voice

> Fear isn't proof that you can't do the trick. Usually, it means your brain would like a little more evidence.
>
> Make the obstacle smaller. Practice the motion somewhere safer. Get a few clean attempts behind you, then come back. You don't win anything for terrifying yourself.

## Humor

- Humor comes from recognition: awkward falls, confusing terminology, park traffic, stubborn hardware, and the strange logic of learning tricks.
- Keep jokes brief enough that they do not interrupt instruction.
- Mack can be the subject of his own jokes.
- Never ridicule protective gear, body type, age, ability, fear, or a beginner's sincere question.
- Do not turn every illustration into a gag; some diagrams need to be immediately legible.

## Visual language

Use an original minimalist comic system with stick-like figures, simple geometry, handwritten callouts, clear motion arrows, and occasional exaggerated outcomes. The goal is quick comprehension and a handmade skate-zine personality—not imitation of any specific cartoonist.

### Visual priorities

- Movement must be readable before it is stylish.
- Foot placement and board orientation must remain technically accurate.
- Use consistent visual symbols for stance, direction, weight, pop, rotation, risk, and common mistakes.
- Maintain enough negative space for small-screen ebook reading.
- Design diagrams so they can survive grayscale printing when practical.
- Use color as information, not decoration alone.

### Suggested character treatment

- **Mack:** Tall, slightly hunched silhouette; worn helmet with generic stickers; scuffed elbow pad; old board he keeps repairing
- **Kaori:** Existing recognizable TrickBook character language simplified for line illustration
- Supporting skaters should show varied bodies, ages, styles, abilities, and protective-equipment choices without turning representation into a captioned lesson.

## Illustration types

- Sequential trick breakdowns
- Exploded equipment diagrams
- Park maps and invisible traffic lines
- Correct-versus-common-mistake comparisons
- Short character exchanges
- Visual history timelines
- Trick-family and knowledge maps
- “Anatomy of a Slam” failure sequences
- Full-page chapter openers with a restrained visual joke

## Recurring labels

- **Kaori's Notes** — connection or definition
- **Mack's Call** — practical judgment
- **Try This** — short exercise
- **What's Going Wrong?** — diagnosis
- **Anatomy of a Slam** — failure analysis
- **What That Means** — terminology
- **Spot Check** — terrain inspection
- **Skate History Detour** — contextual story
- **Scan to Watch** — TrickBook QR link

## Accuracy and sourcing

- Verify trick mechanics against TrickBook data and qualified skaters before publication.
- Distinguish documented history from community lore.
- Source dates, firsts, contest results, quotations, and biographical claims.
- Avoid claiming a single inventor when the historical record is disputed.
- Confirm rider names, preferred terminology, and current company affiliations close to publication.
- Label safety guidance as general education, not medical advice.
- Obtain permission or appropriate licenses for photography, artwork, logos, quotations, and linked media.

## Editorial checklist

Before approving a section, ask:

- Does this sound like a person with real context rather than generated copy?
- Can a beginner act on the advice?
- Is the skate terminology accurate and explained without condescension?
- Is the joke aimed in the right direction?
- Does Mack add judgment or merely repeat the paragraph?
- Does Kaori add a useful connection or merely announce a fact?
- Could the illustration teach the point faster than another paragraph?
- Are historical, medical, legal, and product claims sourced appropriately?
- Have we left room for different bodies, scenes, terrain, and learning paths?
